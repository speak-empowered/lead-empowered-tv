Real Leadership Begins When the Plan Falls Apart

By Nathan R. Mitchell, MBAForbes Coaches Council Contributor · Originally published September 10, 2026
Originally published by Forbes Coaches Council on September 10, 2026. Republished with the author's permission. Read the original on Forbes.

After spending more than 25 years in leadership, there’s one thing I know to be true—few leaders wake up expecting everything to go according to plan. There are shifts in the market, budget fluctuations, changes in priorities and the inevitable reorganization.

At the end of the day, sometimes despite years or even decades of strong performance, careers and organizations alike can take an unexpected turn. Leadership isn’t built for these moments but is revealed in them.

I experienced one of these moments myself. After spending more than seven years helping an organization strengthen its leadership, improve communication and build a healthier workplace culture, I found myself among hundreds of other professionals affected by a large-scale reduction-in-force.

Like many people who have experienced an unexpected career change, I had a choice. I could allow the circumstance to define me, or I could let it refine me. I chose the latter. As leadership expert John C. Maxwell has said, “Change is inevitable. Growth is optional.” That experience taught me something I now share with leaders across a wide variety of industries: The greatest leadership test isn’t whether plans succeed. It’s how we respond when the plan no longer exists.

Uncertainty Is The New Leadership Environment

For many organizations today, uncertainty is no longer the occasional disruption. Whether we are looking at workforce or organizational dynamics, they are constantly fluid, and as a result, it’s a constant operating condition that must be navigated carefully and strategically.

Whether we like them or not, economic volatility, changes in workforce expectations, emerging technologies and global events continue to shape how organizations operate. And as leaders, we often find ourselves making decisions with incomplete information while simultaneously trying to provide confidence to our teams and those we lead.

It’s important to point out that employees understand that leaders don’t have every answer, nor do they expect us to. However, what they do need is someone who can provide steadiness when circumstances feel unstable. Leadership isn’t about predicting the future. It’s about helping people navigate through it effectively and with confidence.

Your Team Takes Its Emotional Cues From You

When uncertainty increases in the workplace, team members naturally look to leadership for signals. This is one of the reasons why it’s so important for leaders to practice emotional stability, especially when times are tough. Team members aren’t necessarily looking for certainty, but they are looking for composure among the organization’s leadership.

And when that doesn’t exist, in one way or another, they will find a way to fill the gaps in communication. They will notice when leaders are inaccessible or withdrawn, whether difficult conversations are avoided or addressed with honesty and transparency. They pay attention to whether setbacks become opportunities for learning or opportunities to blame others. In many cases, a leader’s emotional steadiness, or lack thereof, becomes more influential than the challenge, change or uncertainty itself.

At the end of the day, in my experience, calmness among leaders creates confidence among teams. Moreover, consistency builds trust, and leadership presence and approachability reduce overall team anxiety in the workplace. Many leaders may see these traits as soft skills. They are not. They’re strategic organizational advantages.

As Roger Enrico, former CEO of PepsiCo, said, “The soft stuff is always harder than the hard stuff.”

Resilience Is A Leadership Practice

Thomas Edison famously said, “I have not failed. I’ve just found 10,000 ways that won’t work.” Too often, we treat resilience like it’s a personality trait. In other words, it’s something we either possess or we don’t. In reality, resilience is something that can be learned, and it’s often forged during those seasons when we feel like the plan has completely fallen apart.

Resilient leaders understand this, and as a result, they choose to operate from a different set of habits. For example, resilient leaders will acknowledge the reality of a situation, but they won’t allow themselves to be consumed by it. Moreover, they’re willing to adapt instead of clinging to outdated plans, due to their willingness to remain curious when circumstances change.

And perhaps most importantly, they continue to invest in their people, even when the future feels uncertain or even bleak.

Resilient leaders create teams that can recover faster from adversity because they trust the person who’s leading them. In my professional opinion, the companies best positioned for the future won’t be the ones with simply the most advanced technology or largest budgets; they’ll be the ones whose leaders consistently create environments where people feel trusted, valued and connected to a shared purpose.

At the end of the day, employees bring more than their skills to work each day. They bring their own uncertainties, their own family pressures and their own financial concerns and hopes for the future. Leaders who recognize the humanity behind performance not only lead well through adversity; they also create empowered cultures that can endure adversity and come out the other side the better for it.

When people believe they matter, they show up differently. They solve problems more efficiently and effectively. And they remain engaged when circumstances become difficult because they trust the people leading them through the fire.

One Question Every Leader Should Ask

Unexpected setbacks, whether personal, professional or organizational, have a way of clarifying what’s really important. Setbacks often remind us that our titles can change, our jobs can be taken away, strategies can shift or fail entirely and carefully constructed plans can disappear overnight.

However, what endures, for the long term, is the impact we have on others. And that’s why I’ve adopted a simple question that guides my leadership each day: Who will be better because I showed up today?

The answer often changes from one day to the next, but the question doesn’t. Because leadership isn’t measured by whether everything went as planned. It’s measured by whether people became stronger because you were there to lead them when everything fell apart.
